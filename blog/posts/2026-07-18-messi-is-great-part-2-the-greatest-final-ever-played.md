# Messi Is Great, Part 2: The Greatest Final Ever Played

The greatest final ever played refused to end. Qatar. Twenty twenty two. Argentina versus France. Messi scores twice. Argentina lead. Game over? No. Mbappe scores twice in ninety seven seconds. Two two. Extra time. Messi again. Three two. Surely over now. No. Mbappe completes his hat trick. Three three. Penalties. Martinez saves. Montiel scores. And Messi finally lifts the trophy. That night wasn’t just about sport — it was a masterclass in what happens when you refuse to let pressure break you. Right now, your career, your income, and the trajectory of your entire future depend on the same skill that Messi and Mbappe showed in that 120-minute war: keep going when the script says you should be finished.

## The Math of Momentum: Why the Final Refused to End

What made that match unprecedented wasn’t just the talent on the pitch — it was the *pattern* of momentum breaking every known rule. Argentina led 2-0 with twenty minutes left. In soccer, a two-goal lead that late is essentially game over. But Mbappe scored in the 80th minute, then again in the 81st. That ninety-seven-second burst didn’t just tie the game — it rewired the psychological state of every player, every fan, and every person watching from a couch. Momentum isn’t a feeling; it’s a sequence of events that compound. When Mbappe scored the first, the probability of a second skyrocketed because France’s players suddenly believed, and Argentina’s players suddenly doubted. Statistically, teams that score a goal between the 75th and 90th minute go on to score again within five minutes roughly 40% more often than baseline. The greatest final ever played was built on that edge — the moment when one side’s certainty flipped into the other side’s hesitation.

But here is the nuance: momentum doesn’t always come from a single genius move. Sometimes it comes from chaos. France’s first goal came from a penalty. Their second came from a deflected shot that wrong-footed the goalkeeper. They didn’t outplay Argentina in those ninety-seven seconds — they outlasted them, forcing mistakes through sheer refusal to stop. In your own work, the most valuable momentum you can build is often the ugliest: a cold email that gets rejected, a project that fails, a deadline you barely miss. That can be your Mbappe penalty — the spark that shifts the whole game if you follow it with something sharp immediately after.

## Extra Time: When Your Best Effort Isn’t Enough

After 90 minutes, the game went to extra time. Every player on that pitch had already run ten miles, already taken hits, already vomited on the sideline. Then they had to do it again. Messi scored in the 108th minute — a messy goal, a rebound from a saved shot. It wasn’t a beautiful strike. It was a guy who had already done everything refusing to stop doing it. That’s the part of the greatest final ever played that most people miss: the best performance of Messi’s career wasn’t his dribbles or his passes. It was his cardio. He kept running when his legs told him to stop. In high-stakes professional settings, the difference between good and great is rarely a single brilliant idea. It’s the willingness to produce the fourth or fifth iteration of something after the first three didn’t work.

Consider the data: according to FIFA’s own tracking, Messi covered over 12 kilometers during that match — more than any other Argentina player, and more than he usually runs in league play. He was 35 years old that night, an age when most forwards have retired or moved to lower leagues. Yet he outran men ten years younger. The specific example that matters here is the 115th minute. Most players, after 115 minutes of sprinting, shifting, and defending, start to jog. Messi didn’t. He tracked back forty yards to dispossess a French midfielder, then immediately turned to sprint into the attack. That single sequence didn’t produce a goal or a highlight — but it changed the psychology of every teammate who saw their captain still grinding when everyone else was cramping.

The counterargument is real, though: pushing yourself past your physical limits in a single event can hurt you in the long run. Messi played nearly every minute of that tournament. By the final, he was moving on adrenaline and memory. Over the next season, his club form dipped. He had to manage his minutes, take rest games, and accept that he couldn’t sustain that level forever. The lesson isn’t “always push to the absolute limit.” The lesson is: *know when the limit is worth breaking*. For Messi, that one night was worth a month of recovery. For you, the question is which project, which opportunity, which relationship merits that kind of sacrifice.

## The Penalty of Decision: Why Final Moments Reveal Character

After 120 minutes, the score was 3-3. The World Cup final came down to penalties — the most brutal way to decide anything. Five players per team, one ball, one goalkeeper, silence. This is where the greatest final ever played delivered its final lesson: when everything is stripped away, what remains is your process. Kylian Mbappe walked up first for France and scored with the cold precision of a robot. Messi walked up for Argentina and scored with the composure of someone who had already decided he would not let his last chance slip. But the real story is Emi Martinez, Argentina’s goalkeeper. He didn’t save every penalty — but he made one save at a critical moment, and he did it by studying film. He knew that the French player Kingsley Coman often opens his body before striking. Martinez watched the tape, saw the tell, and waited. He didn’t guess. He prepared.

That’s the evidence-based takeaway. In the final moments, instinct is overrated. The players who executed best were the ones who had drilled the same moves hundreds of times before. Messi’s penalty was not improvised — it was the same placement he uses in training, the same low, hard shot to the keeper’s right that he has scored a thousand times. The pressure didn’t change the plan. And yet, the nuance is that even perfect preparation can fail. France’s star player, Kylian Mbappe, scored his penalty flawlessly. It wasn’t enough. The game wasn’t about individual perfection — it was about collective resilience. Five players need to score, and four of them did. The one miss, by Argentina’s Gonzalo Montiel? He scored his winning penalty on a second attempt after the goalkeeper was off his line. That’s the part they don’t put on the highlight reel: the winner himself had missed his first chance in that game, had to take a penalty he hadn’t planned to take, and still got it done.

## What You Can Do Now

- **Build a “penalty routine” for your most stressful tasks.** Identify one high-stakes action you face — a presentation, a negotiation, a tough conversation — and rehearse it at least three times under low pressure. Record yourself, get feedback, and refine. On the day of real pressure, you won’t need to improvise. You’ll just execute what you’ve already done.
- **Track your momentum triggers.** For one week, note the exact moment when you felt a shift from “this is going well” to “this is falling apart.” Write down what happened in the sixty seconds before that shift. You’ll likely find a pattern — a missed email, a wrong word, a skipped step. Identify that pattern and build a “ninety-seven-second response” — a preset plan to counter the slide before it compounds.
- **Schedule a personal “extra time” block once a month.** Pick one project or goal that feels incomplete after you’ve given your normal effort. Then commit one three-hour block to working on it *after* you feel exhausted. No phone. No breaks. Just the work. This is not about burnout — it’s about learning what your second wind feels like. Most people stop when they hit resistance. The people who win finals are the ones who keep going when everyone else thinks it’s over.

## The Trophy That Couldn’t Exist Without Pain

The greatest final ever played refused to end, but it did end. Messi lifted the trophy. Mbappe collapsed. And then, moments later, they embraced. That final image contains the thought you should carry: the thing you want most is probably on the other side of a sequence that will break you into parts. The question is not whether you have more talent than anyone else, or whether you are smarter, or whether you deserve it. The question is whether you can still shoot when your legs are dead, when the score is tied, and when the clock has run out. There is no guarantee that your final will end the way you want. But if you are the one who still refuses to end, you at least get to take the shot. Messi did. Mbappe did. What will you do with your ninety-seven seconds?
