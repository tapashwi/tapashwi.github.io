# The Hormuz Strait controls 20% of the world oil supply

21 miles. That’s what separates you from Iran’s coast. You could swim that in a few hours, or drive it, in theory. But nobody’s taking a casual dip here. This is the Strait of Hormuz, and that narrow 21-mile gap might be the most consequential bottleneck on Earth. It’s not just a shipping lane; it’s a geopolitical pressure point where the world’s energy security hangs by a thread. To understand the modern global economy, you have to understand this treacherous waterway.

## The 20% Lifeline: An Unreplaceable Artery

The most critical fact about the Hormuz Strait is one of sheer volume. Roughly **20% of the world’s total oil supply** flows through this narrow chokepoint between Iran and Oman. According to the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA), that translates to about 17 to 20 million barrels of oil per day—enough to keep the lights on in major cities and the engines running in global transportation networks. This isn’t a minor side route; it’s the main artery for global energy. Major oil-producing nations like Saudi Arabia, Iraq, Kuwait, and the United Arab Emirates rely almost exclusively on the strait to get their product to market. Even with modern pipelines, there is simply no alternative route that can handle this massive volume. The strait is irreplaceable, making any disruption an immediate global crisis.

## Beyond the Ships: A Minefield of Military Tensions

The real threat isn’t just the possibility of a ship running aground; it’s the military tensions simmering on both shores. The strait is flanked by Iran on the north and a coalition of Arab states, many hosting U.S. military bases, to the south. Iran has repeatedly threatened to close the strait in response to sanctions or military pressure. Its naval forces regularly conduct provocative exercises in the waterway, demonstrating their capability to mine the strait, launch fast-boat swarm attacks, or use shore-based missiles. For example, in 2019 and 2020, a series of mysterious attacks targeted oil tankers and energy facilities in the region, escalating fears of a direct conflict. A blockade here doesn’t require a full-scale war. A single act of sabotage, a naval skirmish, or even a credible threat of mining could be enough to send insurance rates skyrocketing and convince tanker operators to seek slower, more expensive routes—immediately constricting the flow of oil.

## The Domino Effect: Your Grocery Bill and Factory Jobs

And here’s what most people aren’t thinking about. A major disruption at the Hormuz Strait doesn’t just mean a temporary spike in gas prices at the pump. It triggers a **global domino effect** that would rattle the very foundations of daily life. First, the price shock in energy would cascade through every sector. Transportation costs for food and goods would soar, directly inflating grocery bills. Natural gas, some of which also transits the strait, is a key feedstock for fertilizers; its price hike would further increase agricultural costs. Factories worldwide, dependent on affordable energy and the global just-in-time supply chain, would face production shutdowns and layoffs. A prolonged closure could force nations to ration fuel, grounding airlines and limiting commutes. Honestly, our modern, interconnected lives are built on the assumption of a steady flow of energy. A month-long shutdown would test that assumption to its breaking point, exposing how vulnerable we all are to this single, strategic chokepoint.
